🖼️ Interactive Image Filtering Sandbox

Compare exact 3×3 cross-correlation (equivalent to convolution for symmetric kernels) with a teaching model that clears the low bits of every product. The displayed kernels are applied without flipping them; Sobel orientation follows that convention. The numbers measure pixel differences only, not whether the result is good enough for a task.

📘 Guided lab: how to use this sandbox

Before you start. You need three things: what a pixel and an 8-bit value are (a whole number from 0 to 255); what a weighted average is; and what it means to compare two images over the same region. If any of those is new, read Why human eyes and pixels forgive inexact math first. No circuit knowledge is required.

The controls on this page, and what each one does.

  • Test Image Pattern — the picture being filtered: high-contrast shapes, a smooth gradient with noise, or a sharp checkerboard grid.
  • Convolution Filter Kernel — Gaussian Blur 3×3, Sobel Edge Detection, or Sharpen Filter.
  • Arithmetic Model — Exact products, or a teaching model that clears the low 4 or the low 6 bits of every product.
  • PSNR (8-bit, inner pixels) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) — the live quality scores, computed over the inner region only (the 1-pixel border has no full neighbourhood, so it is not filtered).
  • Hardware Energy — permanently "Not measured". This page never measures circuit energy, and no setting changes that.

Three tasks. Predict on paper, then operate the controls, then explain.

  1. Task 1 — does a fine grid forgive truncation? Set Test Image Pattern to Sharp Checkerboard Grid and leave Arithmetic Model at Exact products. Predict whether PSNR will be high. Now change only the Arithmetic Model to clear 4 LSBs. Predict whether PSNR drops a little or a lot, then check. Explain: why a fine alternating pattern is harder on a truncating model than a smooth ramp is.
  2. Task 2 — whose fault is the score? Set the pattern to Smooth Gradient & Noise and compare clear 4 LSBs with clear 6 LSBs: predict which loses more, then check. Now switch the kernel to Sobel Edge Detection and repeat. Explain: you changed the kernel, not the arithmetic model — so why did the score move?
  3. Task 3 — quality versus cost. Find any combination whose PSNR stays above 40 dB. Predict what happens to the Hardware Energy card. Explain what its answer tells you about the difference between a quality score and a cost measurement.

A static worked example (do this one with a pencil). "Clearing the low 4 bits" means forcing the last four binary digits of a number to 0. The exact product 23 × 7 = 161 is 10100001 in binary; clearing the low 4 bits gives 10100000 = 160, an error of −1. For 27 × 5 = 135, which is 10000111, clearing the low 4 bits gives 10000000 = 128, an error of −7. The rule is the same in both cases; the size of the error is not. Every error under this teaching model is negative, because clearing bits can only lower a product — so these errors are biased in one direction rather than cancelling out. That is why the score changes smoothly rather than staying put.

This sandbox is a teaching model of one arithmetic change inside a filtering calculation. It is not a circuit simulator, it does not measure energy, and its PSNR is not a claim about what a viewer sees.
